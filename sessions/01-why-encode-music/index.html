    1. Why encode music? From notation to machine-readable scores¶

    Session 1, 15 October 2026 (double session, 2 × 90 minutes).

    Every notation is already an encoding. It was made for a particular reader and a particular use, and it writes down some things while leaving others to the reader's knowledge. Computers, and now language models, are readers without that knowledge, so music encoding formats must state explicitly what human readers infer. Formats differ for the same reason historical notations differ: they serve different users and tasks.

    How to use this page

    The demos on this page run in your browser; nothing needs to be installed. They load Verovio (about 12 MB) the first time you use them. The same demos come as Jupyter notebooks, where you can change the inputs and keep your work: see Notebooks at the end.

    What is Verovio?

    Verovio is the program that turns an encoding into a score you can see on this page. It engraves music: it reads an encoded score and draws it as notation (an SVG image). It is built around MEI and converts the other formats on the fly: kern, MusicXML, ABC, Plaine & Easie, EsAC, and the chant formats GABC and Volpiano. It is free and open source (LGPL licence), and the RISM Digital Center leads its development.

    Verovio is written in C++ and comes in versions for other languages. This page uses the JavaScript version, which runs inside your browser; the notebooks use the Python version (pip install verovio). Both are version 6.3.0, so page and notebooks show the same score. You can also try it in the Verovio editor, and CAMAT, the library we use from session 2, renders scores with it.

    Verovio only shows what the encoding says. When a score looks wrong, look at the encoding first, and then at how Verovio reads it: parts 8 and 10 do both.

    By the end of the session you can

    1. explain why a score made for human readers is not enough for a computer;
    2. ask three questions of any notation or format: Who reads it? What does it record? What does it leave out?;
    3. tell a scan, a PDF, an encoded score and a facsimile-linked edition apart, and say who can use each;
    4. place key systems on a timeline from mechanical instruments to kern and the Essen folksong collection;
    5. edit a short ABC, kern or MEI text and render it with Verovio;
    6. turn a melody into a 0/1 grid and say what the grid loses;
    7. check an LLM-written melody against what was asked for.

    2. Why encode music?¶

    • Notation is a selection made for a reader who knows the conventions: performance practice, tuning, how to realise an ornament, how repeats work.
    • A computer knows none of this. A scanned score is an image. To search, analyse, render or play music, a machine needs symbols with explicit meaning.
    • Written and performed differ. One written sign (a trill, a grace note, a fermata) stands for performed events that look different from what is written. MEI can keep both, for example @dur (written duration) next to @dur.ges (performed duration).
    • Three questions to ask of every system this semester:
      1. Who reads it? A singer, a lutenist, a cataloguer, a machine, a language model.
      2. What does it record? Pitch, duration, physical action, sound, structure, layout, metadata.
      3. What does it leave out, or leave to the reader?

    A digital edition (MEI) encodes what a source says. Analysis (kern, note tables, piano rolls) treats notes as data. The same encoding has to serve both, and that is what this course is about.

    3. Scores today: scans, PDFs and facsimile-based editions¶

    Where do you meet scores today? In library digitisations (for example the digital collections of the Bayerische Staatsbibliothek), in PDF archives such as IMSLP, in publishers' editions, and in a few encoded editions. Most "digital" music is a picture of a page.

    Form What the computer has You can… You cannot…
    Scan (TIFF, JPEG; often served through a IIIF viewer) pixels zoom, compare sources, cite a page search for a melody, play it, transpose it
    PDF page images in a container, at most a text layer download, print, share the same as a scan: the notes are still pictures
    Encoded score (MusicXML, MEI, kern) symbols with meaning render, play, search, analyse see what the source looked like, unless it is linked
    Facsimile-linked edition (MEI with <facsimile>) symbols and the page images, linked bar by bar all of the above, and check every bar against the source —

    Access: who may use a score? That depends on rights: in the EU, a composer's works are free 70 years after the end of the year of their death, but an edition, an arrangement or a lyricist can still be protected, and countries differ.

    Showcase: the eu-pd-composers viewer

    The eu-pd-composers viewer (data and code on GitHub) applies the EU rule to the 3,369 composers on Wikipedia's List of 20th-century classical composers, with life dates from Wikidata:

    • 777 are public domain in the EU now (they died in 1955 or earlier), 1,870 are not yet, and 722 have no death date on record. 21 more follow on 1 January 2027.
    • 1,254 are matched to their IMSLP category, with links to 29,033 IMSLP work pages; 20,391 of those pages belong to composers who are public domain now.
    • You can filter by instrumentation, style, citizenship, popularity and the year the works become free.

    Figures from the published viewer (dump r019, checked on 2026-10-14). The status is a heuristic based on the composer's death year only, not legal advice: it cannot see lyricists, editions, arrangements or posthumous publications. Check the work itself before you copy, perform or publish it.

    Try it: whose music becomes free in the EU on 1 January 2027? Does IMSLP have scores of it?

    Accessibility: who can use a score? A screen reader cannot read a scan aloud, a scan blurs when enlarged, and it cannot be turned into music Braille or played back. An encoded score can be all of these (music Braille comes up again in part 4).

    Facsimile-based editions keep the source and the encoding together. MEI stores the page images in a <facsimile> element: one <surface> per page, one <zone> (a rectangle) per bar. Each bar of the music points to its rectangle:

The encoding stays tied to its witness: every bar can be checked against the page, the source's page and system breaks are kept, and the editor's reading sits next to what the source shows. A traditional critical edition replaces the source with a new engraving and moves the differences into a critical report.

    This is where the course is going. The tools of CAMAT, which we use from the next sessions on, grew out of encoding volumes of Denkmäler deutscher Tonkunst from the Bayerische Staatsbibliothek's scans: optical music recognition (OMR) page by page, correction in mei-friend, automatic checks, and measure zones that link every bar back to the scan.

    Demo E: a facsimile-linked page¶

    The first page of Dietrich Buxtehude's Sonata I in F major, Op. I, for violin, viola da gamba and harpsichord, as printed in Denkmäler deutscher Tonkunst, vol. 11 (1903). Left: the Bayerische Staatsbibliothek's scan, with the zones from the MEI file drawn on it. Right: the same MEI file rendered by Verovio, with the page's three systems. Point at a bar on either side to find it on the other; click to compare it close up. Tick Show the bar's MEI to read how the selected bar is encoded.

    Look at bar 11. The 1903 print has the violin's C♯ as a sixteenth note; the encoding follows the first edition, printed in Buxtehude's lifetime, and makes it an eighth note, and an <annot> in the MEI file says so. With Show the bar's MEI ticked you can read it in the code: <staff n="1"> is the violin, n="2" the viola da gamba and n="3" the harpsichord's bass line; each <harm> holds a figure of the figured bass; and the <annot> points at the C♯ with plist. The scan stays the witness, and the encoding records where and why it departs from it. (The note calls the first edition "the 1696 print"; sources date Op. I to about 1694 and Op. II to 1696, so treat the year with care.) The full workflow (OMR, correction, checks) comes later in the course.

    When the reader is a machine, notation becomes a program: positions on a cylinder or holes in paper that trigger sound.

    Demo A: Debussy plays Children's Corner¶

    Debussy recorded Children's Corner himself for the Welte-Mignon (roll 2733). Stanford's SUPRA archive scanned the roll; you can see it in Stanford's viewer. SUPRA turned the scan into two MIDI files:

    • raw: every hole becomes an event: 33,508 holes in 4 tracks (bass and treble notes, and two tracks of expression holes that control loudness);
    • expressive: holes merged into notes and the expression holes turned into loudness and pedalling: 6,031 notes.

    Compare the two versions over the same seconds. A long note on the roll is punched as a chain of holes: at 5.1 s the C3 is a 0.1-second hole in the raw file but a 3-second note in the expressive file. The expressive file joins such chains: the 28,365 holes in the raw file's two note tracks become 6,031 notes.

    Is a roll a score, a recording or a program? Where are the bar lines and the quarter notes? What is a "note" when 33,508 holes become 6,031 notes?

    The 1960s needed music for printing (DARMS) and cataloguing (Plaine & Easie). The 1980s brought corpora for analysis (Essen, MuseData, kern). Folk song made the first large data sets because single melodic lines were simple enough to encode at scale. After 1999 came MEI (Perry Roland's XML schema, built on the principles of TEI) for editions, and from 2000 MusicXML (Recordare) for exchange between notation programs.

    9. Binary notation: holes, grids and what survives¶

    The roll in Demo A is binary too: there is a hole at a position, or there is not. But a roll is an instruction for a machine: its time runs on continuously along the paper, and a long note is punched as a chain of holes. The grid below is something else: an array that a program computes from a list of notes, with one row per pitch and one column per time step (1 = the pitch sounds). CAMAT builds this kind of grid later in the course to search for patterns. Which bars of Alle meine Entchen can you no longer tell apart?

    • Bars 2 (two half notes), 3 (four quarter notes) and 4 (one whole note) all become a run of four cells. Pure occupancy loses where one note ends and the next begins.
    • Filling the grid needs a rule. The demo has two, under Rules. This demo's rule asks what sounds at the start of each cell: a note shorter than a cell can fall between two cell starts and disappear, and an eighth note on the beat grows to a quarter. CAMAT's rule asks what sounds at any time in the cell: no note is lost, but every note grows to fill whole cells, so on a quarter-note grid two eighth notes in one cell sound together, as a chord. Add eighth notes to the melody and switch the rules. Finer grids keep more but cost size; CAMAT's default cell is a sixteenth note.
    • Reading the grid back needs a rule too. Both rule sets read a run of 1s in one row as one held note, so 1111 comes back as a whole note in bars 2, 3 and 4 alike. The opposite rule, "every 1 starts a new note", would give only quarter notes: bar 3 right, bars 2 and 4 wrong. Without onsets, no rule gets all three right. This demo reads the grid column by column, as a sequence of chords, which works for one melodic line but cuts a note that is held while another pitch moves. CAMAT reads each row on its own. Click the C4 cells in the rest of bar 1 to hold the C under D, E and F, then switch the rules.
    • What would keep the note boundaries? A second grid that marks where notes start ("keep an onset grid", with this demo's rules), or a note table next to the grid, as CAMAT keeps.
    • Two grids are one grid with three values. With the onset grid ticked, each cell is in one of three states: 0 = silence, 1 = a note continues, 2 = a note starts. Two yes/no grids would allow four combinations, but "a note starts where nothing sounds" cannot happen, so three are left: the notation is no longer binary but ternary. Every cell that is not 0 still says "sounding", so a tool written for the 0/1 grid can read the new grid too. CAMAT has no onset layer yet, so the box is greyed out under CAMAT's rules; CAMAT keeps the onsets in its note table.
    • CAMAT does not read its grid back as the music. It slides a small grid, such as a motif, over the grid of a whole piece and scores each position by the cells they share. For every 1 it can keep a pointer to the notes in the score behind it, so a match is shown in the original score, not in a score rebuilt from 0s and 1s. Statistics such as how often each pitch occurs come from its note table, not from the grid. CAMAT's notebooks binary_roundtrip.ipynb and binary_pattern_search.ipynb show both.

    10. An LLM writes ABC¶

    ABC is compact text, and there are many tune collections online in it, so language models have seen a lot of it. We ask a model for a melody and check the answer. Because the answer is an encoding, we can test it, which a picture of a score would not allow.

    Does every bar add up to the metre? Does Verovio warn? Are the key and the range what was asked for? Is the kern valid at all? Note that the checks rely on how Verovio reads the text: Verovio 6.3, for example, ignores a length written after a chord ([DFA]2), and the page warns when it sees one.

    You do not need your own LLM account for this: the recorded answers and the instructor's demo are enough.

    Addendum: MIDI on a cable and in a file¶

    MIDI comes up in Demo A and in part 7. The name is used for two things: messages sent between instruments, and files that store them.

    On a cable: messages, sent as they happen. MIDI 1.0 was published in August 1983 so that instruments from different makers could control each other. A message says what a player does, not what it sounds like: channel 1, key 60 down, velocity 90 is three bytes, 90 3C 5A, and a second message follows when the key is released. The receiving instrument makes the sound; MIDI itself carries no audio. The original cable has five-pin DIN plugs, carries messages in one direction only (from an OUT to an IN socket) at 31,250 bits per second, and has 16 channels, so one cable can play 16 instruments or sounds. A message carries no time: the note starts when the message arrives, and each three-byte message takes about a millisecond on that cable. Today the same messages also travel over USB, Bluetooth and networks, or between programs on one computer.

    Here is Alle meine Entchen on the cable, as a sequencer sends it when it plays the file further down. A MIDI monitor, a program that lists the messages coming in, shows each one with the moment it arrived. That time is the monitor's; the cable carries only the bytes.

    That is the whole tune: 22 messages, 66 bytes, eight seconds. Nothing in it says bar, quarter note or tempo. A G lasts one second because its key-up arrives one second after its key-down. A person playing the tune on a keyboard sends the same kinds of messages, at less even times and with a different velocity for each key.

    The MIDI specification also has messages it calls real-time messages, in a narrower sense: one-byte messages that keep several machines in step. A sequencer that drives a drum machine sends FA (Start), then F8 (Timing Clock) 24 times per quarter note, at 120 quarter notes a minute one every 21 milliseconds, and FC (Stop) at the end. They carry the beat, but no notes.

    In a file: the same messages, each with a wait before it. A Standard MIDI File (.mid) stores the messages and writes before each one how long to wait after the previous one, counted in ticks. The header says how many ticks make a quarter note, and a tempo message says how long a quarter note lasts. Verovio writes the four bars of Alle meine Entchen as a file of 144 bytes. Here is all of it:

    4D 54 68 64  00 00 00 06       "MThd": the header, 6 bytes follow
00 01  00 02  00 78            format 1, 2 tracks, 120 ticks per quarter
4D 54 72 6B  00 00 00 0B       "MTrk": track 1, 11 bytes follow

  00     FF 2F 00              wait 0, end of track
4D 54 72 6B  00 00 00 67       "MTrk": track 2, 103 bytes follow

    The numbers are hexadecimal: 77 is 119, 3C is 60, and the tempo 07 A1 20 is 500,000 microseconds per quarter note, 120 quarter notes a minute. A wait longer than 127 ticks takes two bytes: 81 6F is 1 × 128 + 111 = 239 (a half note), 83 5F is 3 × 128 + 95 = 479 (a whole note). In 90, the 9 means "key down" and the 0 channel 1. Each key goes up one tick before the next key goes down, so the two Gs of bar 2 are struck twice, not held as one note. The 90 messages are exactly the ones on the cable above; only the waits are new. The FF messages (tempo, time signature, end of track) exist only in files; on a cable, FF alone means Reset. A key "down" at velocity 0 counts as released: the MIDI 1.0 specification allows both this and a separate Note Off message.

    The three questions, asked of MIDI.

    • Who reads it? Instruments, sequencers and recording software; SUPRA turned the Debussy roll into MIDI files.
    • What does it record? Which key, how hard (velocity, 0 to 127), when, on which channel, and controllers such as the pedals: the pedal bands in Demo A are Control Change messages 64 (sustain pedal) and 67 (soft pedal).
    • What does it leave out? What a score adds for a reader. C♯ and D♭ are the same key, 61. A note lasts so many ticks; it has no note value, so a short quarter note and an eighth note followed by a rest can look the same. There are no stems, beams, voices, ties, slurs or staff layout.

    Why MIDI does not simply turn back into MEI. Turning a score into MIDI is easy: Verovio does it, and there is one right answer. The way back is not. Verovio itself cannot read MIDI (6.3.0 answers "Input format 'midi' is not supported"). A program that imports MIDI into a score has to put back what the score had and the file dropped, and that means guessing. Even for the file above, which a computer wrote from a score, a converter has to decide:

    • Note values. The file says "119 ticks", not "quarter note". Here rounding is easy, because every note is one tick short of a quarter, half or whole note. A person playing the same tune holds the keys for uneven times and drifts in tempo, so a converter must first find the beat and then round each length to a note value (quantisation). Is a short note a staccato quarter, or an eighth followed by a rest? Are three quick notes a triplet or a dotted rhythm? In Demo A's expressive Debussy file, the tempo changes all the time.
    • Metre and bars. The cable carries no bars at all. A file may contain a time signature (Verovio writes FF 58, 4/4) or not. Does the tune start on the first beat, or with an upbeat?
    • Pitch spelling. Key 61 is C♯ or D♭, key 70 A♯ or B♭. The converter has to guess the key and spell each note from it. A file may contain a key signature (FF 59), but it does not have to; Verovio wrote none for this tune.
    • Voices and staves. A piano part is one stream of keys. Which notes belong to the melody and which to the accompaniment? Which go on the upper staff and which on the lower? Where does a voice need a rest?
    • What MIDI never had. Slurs, dynamics (only velocities: 90 for every note here), articulation, beams, layout, and ornaments: a played trill arrives as many short notes. What was not recorded cannot be converted back.

    Each of these questions has several possible answers, and different programs answer them differently. Demo C's "Back from the grid to notes" in part 9 is a tiny converter of this kind. It is given the metre, writes every black key as a sharp, chooses the treble or bass clef from the middle pitch, puts all notes into one voice, and needs the onset grid to keep repeated notes apart. MIDI does not need that last help: every note has its own key-down and key-up.
